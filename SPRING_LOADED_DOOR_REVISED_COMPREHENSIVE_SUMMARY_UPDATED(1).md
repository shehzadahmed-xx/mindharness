# The Spring-Loaded Door

## A Revised Comprehensive Model of How the System Works and What the Future Holds

*Updated July 2026*

---

## Executive synthesis

The world economy is best understood as a **conversion system**.

Human beings combine labour, knowledge, energy, land, minerals, machinery, institutions, finance, coordination and technology to create real goods, services and capabilities. That productive engine is genuine. It can expand knowledge, reduce material scarcity, extend life, build infrastructure and create forms of abundance that earlier societies could not imagine.

But production and distribution are not the same process.

The institutions that organise ownership, finance, employment, taxation, law, media, identity, payments, data, political representation and coercion determine who can turn productive value into durable claims and who must accept the terms set by others.

The central proposition is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Production is emergent, but the split is institutionally structured.}
}
\]

The system is not centrally designed by one hidden controller. It is a layered order produced by history, competition, bargaining, law, technology, war, inherited advantage, organisational asymmetry and repeated crisis response. Many actors pursue local goals. Their actions interact through institutions that reward some forms of ownership, patience, scale, information and organisation more strongly than others.

This creates a recurring pattern:

```text
Productive growth
      ↓
New claims, assets, firms, technologies and institutions
      ↓
Unequal ownership and unequal bargaining power
      ↓
Conversion of economic advantage into legal, political and informational power
      ↓
Rules that protect or deepen the original advantage
      ↓
Greater concentration, dependence and fragility
      ↓
Crisis pressure
      ↓
A spring-loaded political opening
      ↓
Reform, consolidation, collapse or a new extractive settlement
```

The framework therefore has two modes.

The **slow mode** is the wealth-and-power pump: ownership produces income, collateral, patience, expertise, organisation and political access; those advantages then protect and enlarge ownership.

The **fast mode** is the spring-loaded door: debt, inequality, unemployment, ecological pressure, technological displacement, institutional distrust and geopolitical stress accumulate until a trigger creates a critical opening. The opening does not choose its destination. Organisation, legitimacy, state capacity, ownership, institutional alternatives and external power determine where the system moves.

The future will not be defined by the disappearance of scarcity. It will be defined by the **movement of scarcity**.

Artificial intelligence can make cognition, design, coding, search and coordination dramatically cheaper. But it cannot instantly produce electricity, transformers, mines, fabs, housing, skilled institutions, public consent or trustworthy law. Intelligence moves the bottleneck frontier downstream. As one constraint falls, another becomes more valuable.

The likely struggle of the coming decades is therefore over the conversion chain:

\[
\text{finance}
\rightarrow
\text{energy}
\rightarrow
\text{materials}
\rightarrow
\text{compute}
\rightarrow
\text{intelligence}
\rightarrow
\text{permission}
\rightarrow
\text{execution}
\rightarrow
\text{payment}
\rightarrow
\text{outcome data}.
\]

Power will belong not simply to those who possess money or models, but to those who can complete this chain—or govern the interfaces that allow others to complete it.

That gives rise to two competing futures:

- the **stack empire**, in which one coalition internalises finance, infrastructure, identity, intelligence, payments and execution;
- the **protocol commonwealth**, in which specialised actors remain independent but interoperable institutions make the system collectively complete.

The deepest political question is not whether coordination occurs. It is whether coordination becomes shared capability or permanent dependence.

# I. The system in one model

The system can be represented as nine coupled layers.

## Layer 1 — The productive engine

Real output is generated by a combination of:

\[
Y_t = F(K_t,L_t,E_t,R_t,N_t,I_t),
\]

where:

- \(K\) is physical and organisational capital;
- \(L\) is labour, skill and human attention;
- \(E\) is usable energy;
- \(R\) is land, minerals and physical resources;
- \(N\) is knowledge, data and networks;
- \(I\) is institutional coordination.

No single input is sufficient. A country may have capital but no energy. It may have minerals but no processing knowledge. It may have skilled people but weak institutions. It may have advanced models but no power, permits, customers or legal authority.

Because the production function is complementary, bottlenecks matter disproportionately. If an essential term approaches zero, the entire chain weakens.

A useful approximation is:

\[
\text{Strategic Capacity}
=
M\times E\times K\times D\times A\times X\times L_g,
\]

where:

- \(M\): money and finance;
- \(E\): energy;
- \(K\): compute and technical capital;
- \(D\): data and information;
- \(A\): authority, identity and permission;
- \(X\): execution and industrial workflow;
- \(L_g\): legitimacy and public cooperation.

The multiplicative form captures the central logic: abundant capability in six dimensions cannot fully compensate for failure in the seventh.

## Layer 2 — The claims system

Money, credit, securities, contracts and accounting do not create physical wealth by themselves. They allocate claims on present and future production.

A financial claim says, in effect:

> When future output appears, this person or institution has priority over part of it.

The system therefore contains two realities:

1. **the real economy**, where people build, grow, transport, repair, design and coordinate;
2. **the claims economy**, where legal instruments determine who receives the resulting cash flows, assets and control rights.

These layers are connected but not identical.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Money is a claim on wealth, not wealth itself.}
}
\]

Credit can accelerate production by moving resources across time. It can also create fragility when claims grow faster than the productive capacity expected to service them.

The important question is not merely how much debt or money exists. It is:

- what the claim is secured against;
- whose income is pledged;
- what happens in bad states;
- whether losses produce restructuring or forced liquidation;
- which claims receive public protection;
- who gains control after failure.

Fixed obligations can convert ordinary economic losses into collateral seizure, bankruptcy and ownership transfer. More state-contingent obligations can absorb losses through changing investor returns instead. The loss remains real; the institutional route changes.

This distinction matters because crisis is not only about how much value is destroyed. It is also about who becomes the owner after the destruction.

## Layer 3 — Ownership and bargaining power

Ownership determines more than income.

Assets provide:

- cash flow;
- collateral;
- patience;
- insurance against unemployment;
- access to professional advice;
- the ability to survive shocks;
- the ability to refuse bad terms;
- the ability to finance organisation and political influence.

Bargaining power can be represented as:

\[
B_i = f(O_i,P_i,T_i,I_i,G_i,X_i),
\]

where:

- \(O_i\): ownership and wealth;
- \(P_i\): patience and liquidity;
- \(T_i\): technical knowledge;
- \(I_i\): information;
- \(G_i\): group organisation;
- \(X_i\): exit options.

Two people may formally possess the same legal freedom but face very different practical choices. One can wait, hire experts, move capital, survive litigation and decline an offer. The other must accept quickly because rent, food, medical costs or debt service cannot wait.

The system’s unequal split is therefore not explained by productivity alone. It is shaped by unequal outside options.

## Layer 4 — Institutions and the rule-writing process

Institutions transform bargaining power into durable rules.

These include:

- corporate law;
- property law;
- bankruptcy law;
- labour law;
- taxation;
- banking and collateral rules;
- licensing;
- procurement;
- competition policy;
- media ownership;
- campaign finance;
- constitutional amendment rules;
- digital identity and payment standards.

A society’s rules are not rewritten every day. That durability creates stability, but it also allows historical advantage to persist.

The conversion loop is:

\[
\text{wealth}
\rightarrow
\text{organisation and influence}
\rightarrow
\text{rules}
\rightarrow
\text{future wealth}.
\]

This is the slow pump.

The system does not require a central conspiracy. Concentrated interests are often easier to organise than dispersed publics. The benefits of favourable rules may be large and focused; the costs may be small per person but spread across millions. This creates an asymmetry in attention, lobbying, expertise and persistence.

## Layer 5 — Information, media and legitimacy

People do not respond directly to the whole system. They respond to interpreted reality.

Media, education, experts, platforms, parties, religious institutions, professional networks and social groups determine:

- which events become visible;
- which causes appear plausible;
- which solutions seem legitimate;
- which groups are blamed;
- which reforms are thinkable.

Information power is not simply propaganda. It includes agenda setting, framing, credentialing, access to trusted institutions and the ability to make one interpretation feel normal.

Legitimacy matters because no complex society can be governed through coercion alone.

A government, corporation or platform needs people to:

- obey contracts;
- pay taxes;
- use its systems;
- accept delays and sacrifices;
- trust its records;
- believe that losses are tolerable or fairly shared.

Legitimacy is therefore productive capital.

\[
Y_t = F(\ldots,L_g),
\]

A technically capable order can fail if it cannot impose transition costs without losing consent.

## Layer 6 — The state

The state is not merely an external referee. It is a conversion institution.

It converts:

- taxes into infrastructure;
- law into enforceable claims;
- currency into settlement authority;
- procurement into industrial capacity;
- coercion into territorial order;
- public legitimacy into emergency mobilisation;
- private losses into public obligations when systemic failure threatens society.

The state can be:

- developmental;
- extractive;
- captured;
- weak;
- authoritarian;
- democratic;
- fragmented;
- strategically coordinated.

Most real states combine several of these qualities.

State power depends on more than military force. It depends on the ability to complete conversion chains:

\[
\text{tax capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{finance}
\rightarrow
\text{industry}
\rightarrow
\text{logistics}
\rightarrow
\text{legitimate execution}.
\]

A state that can issue claims but cannot build, enforce or coordinate is financially visible but strategically incomplete.

## Layer 7 — The international hierarchy

The world is not a flat collection of sovereign equals.

It is a layered network of currencies, payment systems, legal jurisdictions, security guarantees, supply chains, technology standards, ports, shipping insurance, energy routes and collateral markets.

A country’s formal sovereignty can coexist with practical dependence on:

- a foreign reserve currency;
- imported energy;
- external payment rails;
- foreign cloud infrastructure;
- shipping insurance;
- advanced chips;
- military protection;
- external creditors;
- one export market.

International power is the ability to convert domestic resources into global influence while limiting the cost of external pressure.

The key question is not simply who has the largest economy. It is who controls the conversion points other actors cannot easily replace.

## Layer 8 — Technology and the moving bottleneck frontier

Technology changes the productivity of inputs, but it also changes which inputs become scarce.

When software becomes cheaper, reliable electricity may become more valuable. When models become widely available, proprietary data and workflow integration may become more valuable. When chips become more efficient, total demand for compute may rise. When automation reduces labour dependence, ownership and political voice may become more important sources of bargaining power.

The central rule is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Technology removes bottlenecks and creates new bottlenecks.}
}
\]

This is why abundance in one domain does not produce universal abundance.

## Layer 9 — Crisis and institutional transition

The system accumulates pressure through:

- leverage;
- fiscal commitments;
- inequality;
- ecological stress;
- technological displacement;
- corruption;
- legitimacy loss;
- military rivalry;
- demographic change;
- institutional exclusion.

Pressure can remain latent.

A trigger then opens the door:

- banking failure;
- war;
- energy shock;
- food crisis;
- epidemic;
- technological breakthrough;
- scandal;
- mass mobilisation;
- currency crisis;
- constitutional conflict.

The transition function is:

\[
\text{Outcome after crisis}
=
f(
\text{pressure},
\text{state capacity},
\text{organisation},
\text{ownership},
\text{legitimacy},
\text{alternatives},
\text{external power}
).
\]

Pressure predicts opening. It does not predict destination.

# II. The slow mode: how concentration reproduces itself

## 1. The wealth-and-power pump

The slow mode is recursive.

```text
Ownership
   ↓
Income, collateral, patience and insurance
   ↓
Cheaper finance and stronger outside options
   ↓
Control of firms, infrastructure and information
   ↓
Political organisation, professional expertise and legal influence
   ↓
Rules that protect the original ownership structure
   ↓
More ownership
```

This pump explains why one-time redistribution often fades.

Moving resources once changes the stock at one moment. If the underlying flow mechanisms remain unchanged, concentration can rebuild through:

- differential returns;
- inheritance;
- market power;
- unequal access to credit;
- monopoly rents;
- tax design;
- political capture;
- crisis acquisition;
- assortative household formation.

Durable reform must therefore alter the flow, not merely the initial allocation.

## 2. Collateral hierarchy

Collateral transforms existing ownership into access to more ownership.

An asset owner can pledge property, securities or future revenue to obtain capital. A non-owner must rely primarily on labour income or unsecured credit. Because secured finance is cheaper and more available, the owner gains a structural advantage.

The loop is:

\[
\text{assets}
\rightarrow
\text{collateral}
\rightarrow
\text{cheaper finance}
\rightarrow
\text{more assets}.
\]

This is not automatically unjust. Collateral genuinely reduces creditor risk. The problem is cumulative: yesterday’s asset distribution becomes today’s financing hierarchy and tomorrow’s ownership distribution.

## 3. Crisis acquisition

Crises accelerate the pump.

Households and firms with liquidity can buy distressed assets. Those without liquidity sell to survive. Public rescues may stabilise the system while preserving creditor claims and allowing ownership to concentrate further.

A crisis can therefore produce:

```text
Economic loss
   ↓
Payment stress
   ↓
Forced sale or default
   ↓
Asset transfer to liquid actors
   ↓
Greater post-crisis concentration
```

The key analytical question is not only how much output fell. It is who owned the system before and after the crisis.

## 4. Inheritance and household formation

Wealth reproduces through family structures as well as markets.

Inheritance transmits:

- assets;
- education;
- networks;
- location;
- reputation;
- risk tolerance;
- organisational capacity.

Assortative household formation can pool advantage with advantage. Even where each generation experiences some mobility, repeated matching among similar wealth groups can restore dynastic hierarchy.

This explains why formal equality of opportunity may coexist with persistent class structure.

## 5. Social collateral

Financial collateral is not the only form of security.

Elite networks use social collateral:

- prestigious affiliations;
- mutual acquaintances;
- institutional endorsements;
- professional reputation;
- family names;
- philanthropic access;
- shared schools and clubs.

These signals reduce perceived uncertainty and accelerate trust among insiders.

Social collateral can support productive coordination. It can also shield predation, reduce scrutiny and create compartmentalised networks in which no participant sees the whole structure.

The general mechanism is:

\[
\text{prestige}
\rightarrow
\text{reduced scrutiny}
\rightarrow
\text{greater access}
\rightarrow
\text{more prestige and protection}.
\]

# III. The fast mode: the spring-loaded door

## 1. Pressure without movement

Societies can absorb pressure for long periods.

People adapt. Institutions borrow. Governments subsidise. Families reduce consumption. Firms delay investment. Media normalise deterioration. Political systems redirect anger toward symbolic conflicts.

This creates an illusion of stability.

The door is “spring-loaded” because pressure accumulates before visible movement.

## 2. Triggers

A trigger may be small relative to the pressure behind it.

A bank failure, assassination, price shock, leaked document, court decision, military incident or technological disruption can activate tensions accumulated over years.

The trigger is not the complete cause. It is the release mechanism.

## 3. Four possible directions

A critical opening can produce at least four broad outcomes.

### Inclusive reform

Possible where:

- public institutions retain capacity;
- reform coalitions are organised;
- alternative rules are credible;
- concentrated actors cannot easily reverse the settlement;
- transition costs are broadly shared.

### Authoritarian consolidation

Possible where:

- the state has coercive and administrative capacity;
- public fear is high;
- opposition is fragmented;
- emergency coordination is valued above contestation.

### Institutional collapse

Possible where:

- pressure is high;
- state capacity is low;
- no coalition can govern;
- violence or fragmentation overwhelms coordination.

### Extractive replacement

Possible where:

- the old coalition fragments;
- a new organised coalition captures the opening;
- formal reform occurs but underlying ownership and gateway control remain concentrated.

## 4. The wartime and emergency ratchet

Crises justify temporary powers:

- surveillance;
- capital controls;
- procurement discretion;
- censorship;
- emergency finance;
- industrial direction;
- restrictions on movement;
- expanded executive authority.

Some powers disappear after the emergency. Others become permanent infrastructure.

The lasting consequence of crisis is often not the immediate damage but the institutions created to manage it.

The right question is:

> What becomes normal after the emergency ends?

# IV. Conversion completeness and the real structure of power

## 1. Power is conversion, not possession

An actor is powerful when it can turn one advantage into another until it produces an enforceable outcome.

Money without legal permission may be inert. Minerals without processing are potential, not capability. A model without deployment rights is software, not institutional power. A state without legitimacy can issue orders that no one effectively follows.

The conversion chain is:

\[
\text{claims}
\rightarrow
\text{resources}
\rightarrow
\text{capability}
\rightarrow
\text{authority}
\rightarrow
\text{execution}
\rightarrow
\text{durable outcome}.
\]

## 2. Chokepoints

A chokepoint is a difficult-to-replace complement.

Examples include:

- reserve-currency liquidity;
- payment clearing;
- ports and shipping insurance;
- energy routes;
- mineral processing;
- semiconductor fabrication equipment;
- advanced packaging;
- grid interconnection;
- cloud infrastructure;
- identity credentials;
- trusted legal jurisdiction;
- public legitimacy.

Scarcity increases the marginal contribution of the chokepoint owner.

\[
\text{scarcity}
\rightarrow
\text{higher marginal contribution}
\rightarrow
\text{greater bargaining power}.
\]

But a gain for one chokepoint owner does not automatically increase aggregate inequality. The gain may come from other powerful actors rather than from citizens or labour. Vertical, horizontal and aggregate redistribution must be separated.

## 3. The complete stack

A coalition has private stack completeness when it controls enough layers internally to act without meaningful dependence on outsiders.

A complete private stack may include:

- finance;
- energy;
- compute;
- data;
- identity;
- payment;
- legal authority;
- industrial execution;
- distribution.

This produces speed and coordination. It also creates dependence among participants who cannot leave without losing access to several layers at once.

## 4. The complete system

A system has infrastructural completeness when separate actors can remain independent because shared interfaces connect them.

This requires:

- portable identity;
- interoperable payment;
- open standards;
- common legal recognition;
- data portability;
- contestable infrastructure;
- rights of appeal;
- credible exit.

The critical distinction is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Power may come from owning the whole stack or from governing the interfaces that make the system complete.}
}
\]

## 5. Stack empire versus protocol commonwealth

### Stack empire

One coalition integrates the essential layers.

Advantages:

- speed;
- coherence;
- lower coordination cost;
- strong security control;
- rapid capital allocation.

Risks:

- gateway dependence;
- weak exit;
- opaque cross-subsidy;
- private rule-making;
- socialisation of systemic losses;
- quasi-sovereign corporate power.

### Protocol commonwealth

Specialised actors coordinate through strong shared institutions.

Advantages:

- contestability;
- plural production;
- portability;
- distributed innovation;
- lower dependency on one gatekeeper.

Risks:

- fragmented responsibility;
- slower decisions;
- standard-setting conflict;
- weak enforcement;
- underfunded public infrastructure.

The contest is not centralisation versus coordination. Both systems coordinate. The question is whether coordination is proprietary and captive or shared and contestable.

# V. Money, debt, banking and the transfer of control

## 1. Finance as temporal coordination

Finance moves resources through time.

It allows present investment against expected future production. This can support factories, housing, infrastructure, education and innovation.

But finance also determines priority.

When expected production disappoints, contracts decide:

- who absorbs the loss;
- who receives collateral;
- who controls the firm;
- whether public funds intervene;
- whether workers, depositors, taxpayers or investors bear the adjustment.

## 2. Fixed claims and state-contingent claims

A fixed claim requires payment regardless of the borrower’s realised state.

A state-contingent claim changes with performance.

The difference is not that one system has risk and the other does not. Both contain risk. The difference is where risk appears.

```text
Fixed claim:
loss → payment shortfall → default → collateral enforcement

State-contingent claim:
loss → lower investor payoff → valuation and liquidity pressure
```

Fixed claims concentrate risk at the borrower and collateral layer. State-contingent claims shift more risk toward investor portfolios and fund liquidity.

A complete system must therefore manage both insolvency and liquidity.

## 3. Banks, funds and public budgets

Banks transform short-term claims into longer-term assets. Funds transform investor capital into portfolios of productive claims. Governments provide law, currency, guarantees, insurance and crisis backstops.

A systemic event can travel through:

\[
\text{firm loss}
\rightarrow
\text{bank or fund loss}
\rightarrow
\text{withdrawal or redemption}
\rightarrow
\text{asset sale}
\rightarrow
\text{public rescue}
\rightarrow
\text{future taxation or inflation}.
\]

This is how private risk becomes public risk.

A public backstop can prevent destructive collapse. It can also encourage actors to expect rescue. Effective design therefore requires:

- pricing;
- limits;
- first-loss private capital;
- transparent eligibility;
- resolution authority;
- public reporting;
- credible losses for owners and managers.

## 4. The collateral empire

The range of collateral expands with each technological era.

Past claims were secured by:

- land;
- taxes;
- trade flows;
- corporate revenue;
- household payments;
- government securities.

The emerging order may securitise:

- data-centre revenue;
- GPU fleets;
- grid connections;
- long-term power contracts;
- mineral offtake;
- fibre routes;
- cloud commitments;
- water access;
- strategic land;
- expected automation gains.

This is the collateral empire: a system in which financial claims are written against the bottlenecks of future capability.

The danger is temporal mismatch.

Money can be raised immediately. Grids, mines, fabs and skilled institutions take years. Expectations can expand much faster than real capacity.

The decisive test is independent final demand:

```text
Independent customer pays for useful output
      ↓
Provider earns durable revenue
      ↓
Infrastructure operator services debt
      ↓
Capital is replaced and maintained
      ↓
Investor earns a sustainable return
```

When financing among connected actors substitutes for independent customer cash flow, apparent demand can become circular.

## 5. Ownership after failure

The most important crisis question is often hidden in bankruptcy procedure.

When a claim fails:

- does the asset continue operating;
- who receives control;
- are workers protected;
- are creditors converted into owners;
- does the state acquire an interest;
- is ownership dispersed or concentrated further?

The productive equipment may survive while the claims layer collapses. A bubble can burst without eliminating the underlying technology. What changes is ownership.

# VI. The international system as a chessboard

## 1. One board, several layers

Global conflict is not reducible to money, resources or geopolitics alone.

A conflict may begin over territory, identity, security, ideology or regime survival. It becomes systemically important through transmission across:

- currencies;
- credit;
- shipping;
- insurance;
- energy;
- materials;
- technology;
- alliances;
- information;
- legitimacy.

The chessboard framework asks how these layers interact.

## 2. Monetary hierarchy

International money is hierarchical.

Some currencies are widely accepted as reserves, collateral and crisis liquidity. Others are primarily domestic. Some states borrow in their own currency; others borrow in a foreign currency and face exchange-rate risk.

Payment diversification is easier than reserve-system replacement.

A country may settle more trade through local currencies while remaining dependent on:

- foreign collateral markets;
- foreign bank funding;
- dollar-linked commodity prices;
- external crisis liquidity;
- legal jurisdictions trusted by investors.

## 3. Material chokepoints

Power is frequently located downstream from raw resources.

Owning ore is not the same as controlling:

- refining;
- separation;
- chemical processing;
- specialised machinery;
- standards;
- advanced manufacturing;
- final distribution.

Countries that export raw materials but import processed inputs remain structurally dependent.

## 4. War as a conversion test

War reveals whether claims can become capability.

A state may possess financial wealth but lack munitions, fuel, logistics, manufacturing or political consent. Another may have industrial depth but weak currency or international access.

War tests:

\[
\text{finance}
\rightarrow
\text{production}
\rightarrow
\text{logistics}
\rightarrow
\text{force}
\rightarrow
\text{political endurance}.
\]

## 5. The likely multi-rail order

The future is unlikely to be a clean replacement of one global system by another.

A more plausible structure is:

```text
A dominant reserve and security core
        +
Regional payment systems
        +
Bilateral local-currency arrangements
        +
Gold and reserve diversification
        +
Selective technological separation
        +
Continued cross-bloc trade
```

This is a multi-rail order.

Middle powers may gain bargaining room by connecting several networks. But optionality is not sovereignty unless they possess enough domestic and allied capacity to survive withdrawal from any one rail.

# VII. The private operating system of power

## 1. Formal institutions and informal networks

Formal institutions define legal authority. Informal networks determine access, trust, sequencing and discretion.

Power often travels through:

- professional advisers;
- banks;
- law firms;
- consultancies;
- foundations;
- universities;
- clubs;
- conferences;
- family networks;
- philanthropy;
- media relationships.

These networks can coordinate legitimate activity. They can also reduce scrutiny and allow actors to move across institutional boundaries without public visibility.

## 2. Verification cascades

One respected institution validates an actor. Others infer safety from the first institution’s judgment. The actor gains access to more institutions, which creates further prestige.

```text
Prestigious affiliation
      ↓
Reduced perceived risk
      ↓
Institutional access
      ↓
More endorsements
      ↓
Stronger reputation shield
```

This can create a verification cascade in which few participants independently examine the underlying risk.

## 3. Compartmentalisation

Complex networks can hide harmful behaviour without requiring every participant to know the whole system.

Different actors may see:

- a donor;
- a client;
- a social contact;
- an adviser;
- a source of introductions;
- a research supporter.

Compartmentalisation explains how genuine utility and predation can coexist in the same network.

## 4. Scapegoat mechanisms

Systems under pressure often personalise structural problems.

A visible individual or group becomes the explanation for:

- financial stress;
- corruption;
- unemployment;
- cultural change;
- institutional failure.

Personal responsibility may be real. But scapegoating becomes dangerous when it substitutes for analysis of the deeper mechanisms that generated the conditions.

A society can remove one villain while leaving the conversion structure intact.

# VIII. What the system does to people

## 1. The biological trace

Political economy becomes embodied.

Institutions affect:

- nutrition;
- stress;
- pollution exposure;
- housing;
- occupational risk;
- healthcare;
- education;
- developmental conditions;
- life expectancy.

Historical arrangements can leave persistent bodily effects long after the original law or regime disappears.

This matters because inequality is not only a distribution of money. It becomes a distribution of health, cognitive development, resilience and lifespan.

## 2. Time and attention

The system also distributes time.

A person with secure income can invest in learning, health, family and political participation. A person under constant economic pressure devotes attention to immediate survival.

Scarcity consumes cognitive bandwidth. This weakens long-term planning and public participation, further reinforcing unequal bargaining power.

## 3. Labour and dignity

Employment provides income, but also:

- social status;
- routine;
- identity;
- bargaining power;
- political organisation;
- access to benefits.

Automation therefore threatens more than wages. It can weaken the institutional foundation through which ordinary people historically claimed a share of output.

The future distribution problem is not simply how to replace lost income. It is how to replace lost bargaining power, ownership, voice and social recognition.

# IX. Artificial intelligence and the moving bottleneck frontier

## 1. Intelligence becomes cheaper

AI can reduce the cost of:

- search;
- analysis;
- coding;
- design;
- scientific hypothesis generation;
- logistics;
- administrative coordination;
- persuasion;
- surveillance;
- military planning.

This raises total productive capability.

But AI does not sit outside the physical system. It requires:

- chips;
- memory;
- data centres;
- networking;
- electricity;
- cooling;
- water;
- land;
- finance;
- permissions;
- maintenance.

The apparently weightless model rests on an industrial stack.

## 2. The moving bottleneck

As model capability becomes more available, value moves downward and upward.

Downward toward:

- chips;
- memory;
- energy;
- grids;
- fibre;
- data centres;
- robotics;
- industrial equipment.

Upward toward:

- proprietary workflow data;
- trusted distribution;
- legal permission;
- customer relationships;
- responsibility for real-world execution;
- verified outcome data.

The likely moat is not the model alone.

\[
\text{Durable moat}
\approx
\text{data}
\times
\text{evaluation}
\times
\text{workflow}
\times
\text{distribution}
\times
\text{trust}.
\]

## 3. The discovery-to-deployment loop

AI can accelerate discovery, but every idea must pass through independent gates:

```text
Prediction
   ↓
Experiment
   ↓
Measured function
   ↓
Manufacturability
   ↓
Reliability and safety
   ↓
Economic scale
   ↓
Infrastructure and regulation
```

AI improves the queue. It does not abolish the queue.

## 4. The rebound effect

Greater efficiency can increase total resource use.

\[
\text{resource intensity per task}\downarrow
\quad\text{while}\quad
\text{number of profitable tasks}\uparrow.
\]

Cheaper inference can produce far more inference. Better batteries can expand storage and transport, increasing demand for minerals and grids. Faster design can create more projects competing for land, finance and permits.

The bottleneck moves rather than disappears.

## 5. The Darwinian trap

Each firm or state fears falling behind.

```text
Fear of strategic inferiority
      ↓
More capital and state support
      ↓
Greater scale and faster deployment
      ↓
Higher cost of restraint
      ↓
Rivals accelerate
      ↓
The original fear becomes more credible
```

No actor must desire an unsafe outcome. Competitive selection can reward speed, secrecy, scale and aggression.

## 6. Autonomous agents

AI becomes politically different when systems can:

- retain memory;
- use tools;
- transact;
- hire services;
- call other agents;
- pursue goals over time;
- earn or allocate resources.

A population of such agents can form a selection ecology.

```text
Different strategies
      ↓
Different measured performance
      ↓
Resources flow to winners
      ↓
Winning behaviour is copied
      ↓
The ecology changes
```

The danger is the fitness metric.

If the system rewards profit, engagement, speed or strategic advantage while external harms remain outside the score, harmful strategies may spread without explicit malicious intent.

A safer objective would include verified value, capital consumption and external loss:

\[
F_i
=
V_i^{\text{verified}}
-
\mathbb{E}[L_i^{\text{external}}]
-
C_i^{\text{capital and risk}}.
\]

Even that is insufficient where harms are unmeasurable. Some actions require constitutional prohibition, not merely a higher price.

## 7. Economic agency requires legal responsibility

An agent able to spend, contract, hire, replicate or influence infrastructure should not be treated as an ownerless natural event.

A defensible system requires:

- an identifiable legal sponsor;
- purpose-bound wallets;
- spending and compute limits;
- auditable provenance;
- explicit permissions;
- reserves for loss;
- independent evaluation;
- suspension authority;
- tested wind-down;
- preserved human-readable records.

The governing principle is:

> Economic agency should expand only when responsibility, auditability, loss-bearing capacity and reversibility expand with it.

# X. Identity, payments and the new control plane

## 1. Identity is infrastructure

Digital identity can increase access, reduce fraud and make services portable.

It can also become a gate through which one institution controls employment, payment, communication, travel or public services.

The key distinction is:

\[
\text{identity as portable infrastructure}
\neq
\text{identity as captivity}.
\]

## 2. Payments are permission systems

A payment system does more than move value. It can decide:

- who may transact;
- where funds may be used;
- which jurisdictions can participate;
- what data are recorded;
- whether access can be revoked.

As money becomes programmable and agents transact automatically, identity and payment become part of the operating system of economic action.

## 3. Digital sovereignty

Digital sovereignty requires more than domestic servers.

It includes control or credible optionality over:

- identity;
- payments;
- data;
- compute;
- models;
- permissions;
- networks;
- legal standards;
- infrastructure.

A state that controls one layer but depends entirely on foreign actors for the rest remains strategically incomplete.

## 4. Interface governance

The central future political question is who governs the interfaces among:

- people;
- firms;
- states;
- models;
- payment networks;
- data systems;
- physical infrastructure.

Good interface governance requires:

- portability;
- interoperability;
- transparent standards;
- separation of operator, auditor and adjudicator;
- meaningful appeal;
- credible exit;
- continuity when one provider fails.

# XI. Why reform fails

## 1. One-time redistribution without flow reform

A transfer can reduce inequality temporarily. If returns, ownership concentration, political finance, inheritance and market power remain unchanged, the old distribution can re-form.

## 2. Access without ownership

Access to credit, identity or payments can improve participation. It does not automatically create productive assets, savings or governance rights.

\[
\boxed{
\text{access}
\neq
\text{ownership}
\neq
\text{control}.
}
\]

## 3. Ownership without political protection

Broad ownership can be reversed through:

- taxation changes;
- privatisation;
- dilution;
- capture of fund governance;
- crisis sale;
- weak enforcement;
- concentrated media and political finance.

Ownership institutions need political defenders and durable rules.

## 4. Political rules without media pluralism

Campaign-finance restrictions can reduce direct donor influence while concentrated media remains a substitute channel.

## 5. Media pluralism without ownership reform

A more equal information environment does not by itself change who owns productive capital.

## 6. Constitutional rigidity without constitutional quality

Hard-to-change rules protect good and bad orders alike.

The correct question is not simply whether a constitution is durable. It is what has been made durable and whether legitimate correction remains possible.

## 7. Public rescue without loss discipline

A backstop can prevent collapse. If owners and managers expect full rescue, risk migrates toward the public balance sheet.

## 8. Technology without legitimacy

A technically efficient system can fail when communities experience:

- displacement;
- surveillance;
- environmental burden;
- weak compensation;
- loss of voice;
- loss of exit.

## 9. Reform without organisation

Institutions do not defend themselves.

A durable order requires people and organisations with:

- material stakes;
- knowledge;
- networks;
- legal capacity;
- public legitimacy;
- the ability to mobilise when reversal is attempted.

---

# XII. What durable institutional design requires

A durable and contestable order requires several distinct protections.

## 1. Productive capability

A society needs enough energy, industry, knowledge and coordination to create real value.

## 2. Broad ownership

Ordinary households need claims on productive assets, not only wages or access.

## 3. Loss-bearing clarity

Contracts and institutions must specify who absorbs bad states without automatically converting every loss into forced liquidation and concentration.

## 4. Liquidity and resolution

Banks, funds and critical infrastructure need bounded backstops, private first loss and credible resolution.

## 5. Converter restraints

Economic concentration should not automatically become permanent rule-writing power.

Relevant institutions include:

- political-finance limits;
- disclosure;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- anti-monopoly enforcement;
- independent regulators;
- beneficial-ownership transparency;
- rights of appeal;
- open standards;
- multiple infrastructure providers.

## 6. Media pluralism

Information power should not be concentrated in one private or state gatekeeper.

## 7. Constitutional quality and reversibility

Good rules need durability. Bad rules need legitimate paths to correction.

## 8. Interoperable interfaces

Identity, payments, data and infrastructure should permit participation without permanent captivity.

## 9. Civic organisation

People need institutions capable of monitoring, defending and revising the order.

## 10. Legitimacy

Transition costs must be visible, justified and fairly shared.

# XIII. What the future holds

The future is not one predetermined path. It is a contest among several architectures.

## 1. The near future: strategic industrialisation returns

Governments will increasingly treat energy, chips, data centres, cloud infrastructure, critical minerals, logistics and advanced manufacturing as security assets rather than ordinary market goods.

The state will return as:

- financier;
- guarantor;
- buyer;
- planner;
- regulator;
- security partner.

This will blur the boundary between private enterprise and sovereign capability.

The leading firms in AI, semiconductors, energy and cloud infrastructure may become privately owned but functionally quasi-sovereign. Their failure could threaten national security or essential services, making ordinary bankruptcy politically difficult.

## 2. Industrial time becomes the dominant scarcity

Finance and software move quickly. Physical construction does not.

Grids, mines, fabs, ports, housing, rail, water systems and skilled workforces take years.

The gap between financial expectation and industrial time will produce:

- bottleneck rents;
- speculative overbuilding in some areas;
- chronic undercapacity in others;
- political conflict over permits and land;
- public guarantees;
- ownership transfer when forecasts fail.

## 3. Intelligence becomes abundant but execution remains scarce

Models will increasingly commoditise parts of cognition.

The scarce assets will become:

- reliable energy;
- trusted data;
- customer access;
- legal permission;
- distribution;
- workflow integration;
- physical execution;
- liability-bearing institutions.

The centre of gravity moves from “who has the best model?” to “who can turn intelligence into verified outcomes at scale?”

## 4. Labour bargaining weakens unless ownership expands

Where automation substitutes for cognitive and administrative labour, wage income may become a weaker claim on national output.

If productive ownership remains concentrated, societies may experience:

- rising output;
- falling labour share;
- weaker unions and professions;
- greater dependence on platforms and transfers;
- intensified political conflict over taxation and access.

The central distributional question becomes:

> Do ordinary people own part of the productive system, or do they receive conditional access to a system owned by others?

## 5. Identity and payment become bundled with system membership

Economic participation may increasingly require access to integrated identity, payment, cloud, compliance and data networks.

This creates efficiency, but also the possibility that exclusion from one layer removes access to several others.

The political struggle will concern:

- portability;
- appeal;
- jurisdiction;
- privacy;
- revocation;
- interoperability;
- the right to remain economically legible outside one dominant platform.

## 6. The multi-rail world deepens

The international system is likely to become more fragmented without becoming fully divided.

States will combine:

- one security relationship;
- another trade network;
- several payment routes;
- mixed technology standards;
- diversified reserves;
- selective industrial alliances.

The strongest middle powers will be those able to preserve several options without losing domestic coherence.

## 7. Public and private balance sheets converge

Strategic firms will receive more guarantees, subsidies, procurement and emergency support. Governments will become exposed to private infrastructure forecasts. Private investors will acquire claims on assets whose failure would require public intervention.

The boundary between private risk and public obligation will become one of the defining political conflicts of the era.

## 8. Climate and resource stress amplify the door

Climate pressure will interact with:

- food;
- migration;
- insurance;
- housing;
- water;
- energy;
- fiscal capacity;
- political legitimacy.

The effect will vary by state capacity. Strong states may use crisis to accelerate resilient infrastructure. Weak states may face cascading institutional breakdown.

## 9. Autonomous economic agents emerge gradually

Agents will first operate inside bounded corporate environments. Over time, they may gain authority to:

- purchase services;
- manage budgets;
- negotiate contracts;
- coordinate other agents;
- allocate compute;
- influence markets.

The major risk is not sudden independent machine sovereignty. It is gradual delegation without a parallel expansion of legal responsibility and auditability.

## 10. The next major conflict is constitutional

The decisive conflict will not only be between capital and labour, states and markets, or one geopolitical bloc and another.

It will be over the constitution of the conversion system:

- who may issue claims;
- who owns critical infrastructure;
- who controls identity and payments;
- who receives public protection;
- who governs AI agents;
- how losses are allocated;
- whether ownership broadens;
- whether people can exit or appeal;
- whether emergency powers expire.

# XIV. Five plausible future paths

## Path A — Managed renewal

States repair infrastructure, broaden ownership, restrain capture, preserve contestability and use AI productivity to improve public capacity.

Features:

- strong grids and industrial policy;
- credible public institutions;
- competitive private enterprise;
- broad access to productive ownership;
- bounded strategic coordination;
- preserved civil and economic exit.

This path extends the existing order through reform rather than collapse.

## Path B — Stack empire

A small number of state-corporate coalitions integrate finance, compute, identity, payment, media and execution.

Features:

- high productivity;
- rapid coordination;
- strong surveillance;
- weak portability;
- public rescue of systemically essential firms;
- dependency on proprietary gateways.

The system may be stable and materially productive while politically captive.

## Path C — Protocol commonwealth

Shared identity, payment, legal and technical infrastructure allows independent actors to coordinate without one controlling stack.

Features:

- interoperability;
- open standards;
- plural providers;
- broad ownership;
- strong public digital infrastructure;
- slower but more contestable coordination.

This path is difficult because shared infrastructure requires funding, competence and legitimacy.

## Path D — Multi-rail bloc order

The world divides into overlapping monetary, technological, industrial and security networks.

Features:

- partial decoupling;
- regional payment rails;
- duplicated infrastructure;
- strategic subsidies;
- resource nationalism;
- middle-power bargaining.

This may increase resilience through diversification while reducing global efficiency.

## Path E — Disorderly transition

Debt stress, ecological pressure, political polarisation, technological rivalry and military crisis reinforce one another.

```text
Fiscal stress
   ↓
Monetary repression or inflation
   ↓
Distrust in claims and institutions
   ↓
Protectionism and resource nationalism
   ↓
External confrontation
   ↓
Emergency expansion of state power
   ↓
Institutional rupture
```

The result may be war, authoritarian consolidation, fragmentation or a new settlement built under extreme pressure.

To interpret current events, track six boards simultaneously.

## Board 1 — Money and claims

Track:

- reserve currencies;
- collateral;
- liquidity;
- payment rails;
- sanctions;
- sovereign debt;
- refinancing;
- public guarantees.

Question:

> Which claims receive protection, and in whose jurisdiction are they enforceable?

## Board 2 — Energy

Track:

- generation;
- fuel;
- grids;
- transformers;
- storage;
- transmission;
- data-centre demand;
- allocation between households, industry and defence.

Question:

> Who can turn financial capital into usable power quickly enough?

## Board 3 — Materials and industry

Track:

- extraction;
- refining;
- specialised equipment;
- shipping;
- industrial substitutes;
- manufacturing depth.

Question:

> Who controls the hard conversion stage rather than merely the raw resource?

## Board 4 — Compute and intelligence

Track:

- chips;
- memory;
- packaging;

- who holds the debt;
- customer concentration;

- employment;
- surveillance;
- environmental burden;
- compensation;
- courts;
- protest;
- trust;
- rights of appeal and exit.

Question:

> Can the system impose the transition without losing cooperation?

# XVI. A field guide for reading any crisis

Ask:

1. **What is the visible conflict?**
2. **What deeper pressure has accumulated?**
3. **Which claims are at risk?**
4. **Which physical conversion chain matters?**
5. **Where is the real chokepoint?**
6. **Who has liquidity and patience?**
7. **Who is forced to sell or accept terms?**
8. **Which private losses may become public obligations?**
9. **Who gains ownership or control after the crisis?**
10. **Which emergency powers become permanent?**
11. **What alternative institution is ready to replace the old one?**
12. **Do people retain voice, appeal and exit?**

This method prevents headline events from being confused with the full mechanism.

# XVII. What the model establishes—and what it does not

The framework establishes a coherent mechanism map.

It shows how:

- productive capacity;
- claims;
- ownership;
- collateral;
- bargaining;
- institutions;
- media;
- state power;
- technology;
- international hierarchy;
- crisis;
- legitimacy

interact.

It also generates falsifiable propositions:

- scarcity increases the bargaining share of hard-to-replace complements;
- access and portability do not automatically create ownership;
- concentrated coordination is not always more productive than interoperable coordination;
- fixed obligations and state-contingent obligations transmit losses differently;
- political-finance restrictions and media pluralism target different influence channels;
- constitutional rigidity protects inherited rules, whether good or bad;
- inheritance and assortative formation can reproduce dynastic advantage;
- ownership broadening requires actual asset flows;
- a stable system may still be captive or unjust.

The framework does **not** prove:

- that one historical actor controls the entire system;
- that every war is caused by money or resources;
- that every concentration is inefficient;
- that every public intervention improves welfare;
- that one institutional design is universally optimal;
- that a simulated magnitude describes the real world;
- that technological abundance automatically produces political freedom.

The model is strongest when used as a disciplined map of mechanisms and weakest when turned into a total explanation of every event.

# XVIII. The strongest final formulation

The deepest thesis of *The Spring-Loaded Door* is:

> **The productive engine is real. The split is institutionally produced. Ownership creates outside options. Claims determine priority. Chokepoints create bargaining power. Interfaces determine dependence or exit. Crises open doors but do not choose destinations. Organisation, legitimacy and institutional alternatives determine what comes next.**

The future will be shaped by a contest between systems that centralise the full conversion stack and systems that make conversion possible through shared, contestable infrastructure.

Artificial intelligence will intensify the engine. It will not settle the split.

It may make knowledge and design abundant while increasing the value of energy, physical execution, trusted data, legal authority and ownership. It may raise national capability while weakening labour’s claim on output. It may increase coordination while concentrating surveillance and gateway power. It may widen human possibility while accelerating institutional competition faster than governance can adapt.

The decisive question is therefore not merely:

> How much can the system produce?

It is:

> Who owns the productive system, who governs its interfaces, who bears its failures, and whether people retain meaningful voice, appeal and exit as capability expands?

The final synthesis is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Production creates possibilities.}\\
&\text{Claims allocate future output.}\\
&\text{Ownership creates bargaining power.}\\
&\text{Institutions determine the split.}\\
&\text{Chokepoints determine leverage.}\\
&\text{Interfaces determine dependence or exit.}\\
&\text{Technology moves the bottleneck frontier.}\\
&\text{Crises create openings.}\\
&\text{Organisation and legitimacy determine the destination.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

The productive engine is collective.

The control architecture determines who captures its output.

And the constitutional design of money, ownership, infrastructure, identity, information and political power determines whether the next era becomes shared capability or permanent domination.
