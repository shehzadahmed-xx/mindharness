# Revised Comprehensive Summary

*The Spring-Loaded Door* models the economy as a productive but institutionally governed conversion system. Human beings combine labour, knowledge, energy, resources, machinery and organisation to generate real and expanding wealth. That productive engine is neither fictitious nor centrally planned in full. Yet the distribution of its output is shaped by historically formed structures of ownership, bargaining power, monetary access, institutional authority and political organisation.

The framework therefore begins from a dual proposition:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Production is emergent, but distribution is institutionally structured.}
}
\]

Productive growth is real, but its benefits are not allocated by a neutral or necessary process. Unequal outcomes are reproduced through interacting labour-market asymmetries, inherited institutional advantages, collateral hierarchies, financial architecture, market concentration, political organisation, legitimating beliefs, coercive capacity and collective-action constraints.

## The slow mode: the wealth-and-power pump

The system’s slow mode is a recursive wealth-and-power pump.

Concentrated ownership generates income, collateral and patience. These provide cheaper access to finance, stronger outside options and a greater capacity to survive shocks. Economic wealth can then be converted into political organisation, expertise, lobbying, media influence, legal advantage and rule-writing power. Those institutional advantages protect or deepen the original concentration.

The loop is:

\[
\text{ownership}
\rightarrow
\text{income and collateral}
\rightarrow
\text{financial and political capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{favourable institutional rules}
\rightarrow
\text{further ownership}.
\]

This process does not require a single central conspiracy. It can emerge from many individually rational actions inside an unequal institutional structure. The system reproduces itself because concentrated interests are easier to organise than dispersed publics, because those with assets can wait longer, and because rules written under unequal bargaining conditions feed back into future ownership.

## The fast mode: the spring-loaded door

The fast mode is the spring-loaded door.

Debt, inequality, unemployment, technological displacement, corruption, ecological strain, institutional distrust and political exclusion can accumulate without immediately transforming the system. The existing order may appear stable even as pressure builds beneath it.

A trigger then creates a critical juncture:

- a financial crisis;
- war;
- a food or energy shock;
- a technological breakthrough;
- a political scandal;
- an epidemic;
- mass mobilisation;
- institutional breakdown.

The opening does not determine its own destination.

The direction of change depends on:

- state capacity;
- coalition organisation;
- institutional inheritance;
- public legitimacy;
- elite cohesion or fragmentation;
- ownership structure;
- alternative institutions;
- coercive capacity;
- external support and geopolitical conditions.

A crisis may produce inclusive reform, authoritarian consolidation, institutional collapse or a new extractive arrangement. Pressure predicts instability more reliably than it predicts progress.

## Productive engine and institutional split

The framework distinguishes the productive engine from the institutional distribution of its output. It therefore rejects two opposite errors.

The first error is that all wealth is merely fictitious or produced only by financial manipulation. Real productive capacity exists and matters.

The second error is that existing allocations follow naturally from production. They do not. Value creation and value capture are different processes.

\[
\text{value creation}
\neq
\text{value capture}.
\]

The unequal split is real but historically contingent. It is reproduced by identifiable mechanisms and can therefore, in principle, be altered.

## Conversion completeness

The framework now treats power as the ability to convert one form of advantage into another until it produces a durable real-world outcome.

The conversion chain is:

\[
\text{finance}
\rightarrow
\text{energy}
\rightarrow
\text{compute}
\rightarrow
\text{information}
\rightarrow
\text{authority}
\rightarrow
\text{execution}
\rightarrow
\text{outcomes}.
\]

An actor may possess money but lack energy, compute, legal permission or execution capacity. A state may possess resources but lack industrial depth. A firm may possess a model but lack customer access, payment authority or operational data.

Power therefore depends not merely on possessing assets, but on completing the path from claims to capabilities.

The computational experiment added an important distinction.

### Coalition-level completeness

One coalition internalises or controls every essential layer. It can convert finance into infrastructure, infrastructure into intelligence, intelligence into authorised action and action into outcomes.

This is the logic of the **stack empire**.

### System-level completeness

Different actors remain specialised, but shared infrastructure connects them. Portable identity, interoperable payments, common legal recognition, open standards, shared data rules and public or federated infrastructure allow incomplete actors to become collectively complete.

This is the logic of the **protocol commonwealth**.

The experiment showed that one dominant coalition does not always need to own every layer. Strong shared interfaces can allow several productive coalitions to operate simultaneously.

The revised proposition is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Power may come from owning the complete stack or from governing the interfaces that make the system complete.}
}
\]

## What the computational experiment established

The formal experiment contained eight actors: Finance, Energy, Compute, Data and Oracles, Identity and Payments, Industry and Workflow, State and Regulator, and Citizens and Labour.

Coalition output depended on seven complementary capacities:

\[
F,\ E,\ K,\ D,\ A,\ X,\ L.
\]

The energy shock reduced effective energy capacity by 35 percent. Federation changed only identity and payment portability, switching costs, shared authority and coordination overhead. No actor received a direct architecture-specific bargaining bonus.

The completed run contained 6,400 core episodes and 2,000 sensitivity episodes across four behavioural adjustment rules.

The results should be interpreted as causal findings inside a formal world, not as estimates of real-world magnitudes.

### Scarcity raises the power of the scarce complement

The energy shock increased Energy’s payoff share without assigning it an artificial bonus.

The supported mechanism is:

\[
\text{scarcity}
\rightarrow
\text{greater marginal contribution}
\rightarrow
\text{greater bargaining power}.
\]

This supports the chokepoint thesis. Actors controlling a difficult-to-replace complement can gain leverage even when they do not control the whole system.

### Private identity and payment control create gateway rents

When identity and payment authority remained proprietary, the private gateway captured a larger share.

When part of that authority became shared and portable, the private gateway’s share declined.

The relevant political question is therefore not simply whether digital identity or programmable payments exist. It is whether they are portable, interoperable and contestable.

\[
\text{identity as infrastructure}
\neq
\text{identity as captivity}.
\]

### Portability improves exit more than ownership

Citizens and Labour gained under federation, but only modestly.

This demonstrates:

\[
\boxed{
\text{access rights} \neq \text{ownership}.
}
\]

Portable credentials and interoperable payments can reduce exclusion and improve outside options. They do not automatically give citizens control over finance, energy, compute, data or industrial workflows.

Broad bargaining power still requires ownership, organisation, savings, technical capacity and institutional representation.

### Concentration is not automatically more productive

The original expectation was that concentrated systems would always coordinate more efficiently.

That claim was falsified in the core calibration.

The federated architecture produced more total output because shared authority allowed several coalitions to function at once, even though each coalition faced greater coordination costs.

The relevant condition is:

\[
\text{net value of federation}
=
\text{bottlenecks removed}
-
\text{coordination costs introduced}.
\]

Federation wins when shared infrastructure is capable enough to become a genuine substitute and when interoperability unlocks more production than coordination consumes.

Concentration can still win when shared infrastructure is weak, fragmented or expensive to govern.

The real institutional contest is therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{proprietary integration}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{interoperable coordination}.
}
\]

### Shocks do not automatically increase aggregate inequality

The energy shock increased Energy’s share but slightly reduced total inequality.

This falsifies the overbroad claim that scarcity shocks necessarily worsen aggregate inequality.

Three processes must be distinguished:

1. **Vertical redistribution:** from weaker actors to stronger actors.
2. **Horizontal redistribution:** among powerful actors.
3. **Aggregate inequality:** the final distribution across the system.

A chokepoint owner can gain at the expense of other powerful actors without increasing overall inequality.

The corrected proposition is:

> A shock increases the bargaining share of the newly scarce complement. Its effect on inequality depends on whose share is displaced.

### Stability is not the same as resilience or justice

The concentrated architecture reached more static equilibria. But it also had higher switching costs and stronger gateway dependence.

A system may be stable because actors prefer it, or because they cannot leave.

\[
\text{static stability}
\neq
\text{resilience}
\neq
\text{welfare}
\neq
\text{freedom to exit}.
\]

A captive order can be stable. A competitive order can be restless.

Future analysis must therefore distinguish:

- equilibrium stability;
- recovery after shocks;
- adaptability;
- welfare;
- contestability;
- voice;
- exit.

## The revised intervention family: \(L_8\)

The framework’s principal intervention family, \(L_8\), targets the conversion of concentrated economic wealth into durable political and institutional control.

Its purpose is not simply to redistribute income once. It is to increase the institutional half-life of reform by preventing concentrated interests from purchasing the power to rewrite rules in their favour.

The computational results broaden \(L_8\).

It should now be understood as a family of **converter-interruption and interface-governance institutions**.

These may include:

- political-finance constraints;
- anti-capture and conflict-of-interest rules;
- transparency of beneficial ownership;
- competition and anti-monopoly institutions;
- independent public-interest regulators;
- portable digital identity;
- interoperable payments;
- open technical standards;
- data portability and consent;
- multiple oracle and infrastructure providers;
- public or cooperative ownership of critical interfaces;
- meaningful rights of appeal and exit.

The objective is not to prevent coordination. It is to prevent necessary coordination from becoming permanent domination.

Existing empirical results do not establish a universal \(L_8\) effect. They define a research programme for identifying:

- which converter-interruption institutions work;
- which interfaces should be shared;
- under what conditions;
- at what coordination cost;
- for how long;
- against which forms of adaptation by concentrated interests.

## Historical persistence and embodied effects

Historical political-economic arrangements may leave persistent bodily and epidemiological effects through:

- nutrition;
- stress;
- toxic exposure;
- developmental conditions;
- occupational hazards;
- disease environment;
- continuing institutional disadvantage.

Such evidence can strengthen claims that historical injustice remains materially present.

It does not automatically establish a quantified legal or financial debt. Causal attribution, institutional succession, claimant standing, valuation and remedy require additional legal, empirical and normative analysis.

The framework therefore distinguishes:

\[
\text{evidence of persistent harm}
\]

from:

\[
\text{a complete theory of liability and remedy}.
\]

## Recurrent institutional configurations

The framework identifies several recurrent configurations.

### Inclusive and contestable configuration

This combines:

- broad participation;
- relatively strong public institutions;
- meaningful exit;
- distributed bargaining power;
- legitimate and interoperable interfaces;
- limits on converter capture.

### Extractive and institutionally vulnerable configuration

This combines:

- concentrated ownership;
- weak bargaining institutions;
- private chokepoints;
- political capture;
- fragile legitimacy;
- limited exit.

### Developmental-authoritarian configuration

This combines:

- high state coordination;
- strong productive investment;
- restricted political contestation;
- concentrated decision authority;
- varying degrees of long-run inclusion or extraction.

### Stack-empire configuration

A private or public-private coalition internalises finance, infrastructure, intelligence, identity, payments and execution.

It may be highly productive and coordinated, but participants depend on its gateways.

### Protocol-commonwealth configuration

Specialised actors remain separate while strong shared interfaces allow them to coordinate.

It may preserve contestability and distributed production, but it risks fragmentation, weak standards and diffuse accountability.

These are not established as independent mathematical equilibria. They are observable patterns generated by one coupled system under different combinations of:

- state capacity;
- ownership;
- distribution;
- legitimacy;
- interoperability;
- political organisation;
- coercive capacity;
- exit.

The system’s coordinates condition transitions without mechanically determining them.

## The purpose of the theory

The aim of *The Spring-Loaded Door* is not to reduce political economy to a single equation.

It is a mechanism-organising framework for explaining how:

- production;
- distribution;
- money and credit;
- ownership;
- political organisation;
- institutional capture;
- crisis;
- psychology;
- technology;
- coercion;
- legitimacy;
- reform

interact across time.

Its purpose is also practical: to identify where interventions can alter the mechanisms that continually reproduce unequal outcomes.

The key intervention points are no longer only taxes, transfers or ownership. They also include:

- collateral rules;
- access to finance;
- public infrastructure;
- identity and payment portability;
- interoperability;
- data rights;
- governance of standards;
- rights of refusal, appeal and exit;
- institutions that prevent economic concentration from becoming permanent rule-writing power.

# The Strongest Final Formulation

The deepest thesis of *The Spring-Loaded Door* is:

> **The productive engine is real. The unequal split is institutionally produced. Scarcity shifts power toward chokepoint owners. Crises open doors but do not choose destinations. Reform fails when concentrated wealth can still purchase the power to reverse it—or when private gateways make exit impossible.**

The deepest policy implication is:

> **Justice is not achieved by moving resources once. It requires institutions that prevent yesterday’s concentration from becoming tomorrow’s rule-writing power, and shared interfaces that allow people and organisations to participate without permanent dependence on a single gatekeeper.**

The final synthesis is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Production creates possibilities.}\\
&\text{Institutions determine the split.}\\
&\text{Chokepoints determine bargaining power.}\\
&\text{Interfaces determine dependence or exit.}\\
&\text{Crises create openings.}\\
&\text{Organisation and legitimacy determine the destination.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

The productive engine is collective.

The control architecture determines the split.

And the constitutional design of the interfaces determines whether coordination becomes shared capability or permanent domination.
